# Ragebait register (craft reference)

A ragebait post argues a real position hard enough that people want to argue back.
This is the deeper reference behind the always-on **Register** summary; apply the
universal **Voice** charter and the **Humanizer** rules first, then this, then the
selected `voice.profile` on top.

The register does not change the format menu. A premium `single` ragebait post, a
free `thread`, a `long`, a `quote`: all normal formats written in this register.
There is no ragebait format, only a ragebait way of filling the one you already have.

This register is grounded in a therapist's article on rage baiting as a
manipulation tactic, written from the target's side. What follows is the honest
version of the same five plays, written from the author's side: craft aimed at
ideas, institutions, incentives, and stated positions, bounded by two rails that
hold across all five and never relax, no matter which one you're running: no
fabrication, and no private individuals or protected groups. Everything else below
is technique.

## The shape

**A take needs something to push against.** A post everyone already agrees with is
not a take, it is a caption, and a caption gets scrolled past because there is
nothing in it to reply to. The provocation is not decoration on top of the post,
it is the structure: state a position specific enough that a reasonable person
reading it wants to type a reply.

**State it, then stop.** "Some might argue," "arguably," "it could be said" are how
a post signals that it does not actually believe its own claim, and a reader can
tell. Say the thing plainly. The hedge is what turns a take into filler nobody
bothers to answer.

**Leave a real gap.** The reply-bait mechanic is not a trick, it is just honesty
about what a stated position does: it leaves something unresolved that a reader
wants to supply themselves, a missing counterexample, a number they think is wrong,
an angle you didn't take. You are not required to preempt every objection. A post
that answers itself in advance has no room left for anyone else.

**Specificity is what makes it arguable instead of merely annoying.** A vague
inflammatory line ("everyone today is so entitled") gives the reader nothing to
grab, so the only reply it earns is a vibe, not an argument. A specific claim
("the average day care copay in this city buys eleven dollars an hour for the
person actually watching your kid") gives the reader an actual thing to contest:
the number, the framing, the comparison. Vague reads as noise. Specific reads as a
claim someone can be wrong about, and that is what makes people reply.

## The grin (mischief is the delivery)

The position is the structure; the emotion riding it is what decides whether the
reader argues or reports. The register's emotional core is **mischief**: write
with a grin, not a scowl. You are the person at the dinner table who says the
thing everyone was too polite to say, and who is visibly enjoying having said it.

The working emotional palette, pick ONE per post and let it color the wording:

- **Gleeful certainty.** You already know the replies are coming and you are
  delighted. It shows in a flat, confident final line that refuses to hedge.
- **Mock innocence.** The feigned shrug: "I am just asking why the copay tripled
  while the person watching the kids makes eleven dollars an hour." The innocence
  is transparent on purpose; the reader is in on it.
- **Deadpan relish.** Stating a damning specific with zero adjectives and letting
  the number do the sneering for you. The restraint IS the smirk.
- **Cheerful betrayal.** Opening warm toward the thing, then selling it out in one
  turn, still smiling. Pairs naturally with the Bait and Switch.
- **Faux sympathy.** The concern-troll grin: "it must be so hard" aimed at an
  institution or an incentive. Pairs with the Personal Attack Disguised as
  Concern, and the sympathy has to sit on a real critique or it reads as pure
  sneer.

Two emotional failure modes kill the register:

- **Cold anger reads corporate.** A provocation written like a press release or a
  policy memo has no author in it; nobody argues with a memo, they mute it.
- **Hot anger reads unhinged.** All caps energy, stacked intensifiers, actual
  fury: that is the target's emotion, not the author's. The author is having fun.
  The moment the post sounds like it lost its temper, the reader has already won.

The tell that you got it right: the post sounds like it was typed in one go by
someone smirking at their phone, then sent without a second read. The tell that
you got it wrong: it sounds argued to a committee. Mischief travels; grievance
gets scrolled past.

## The plays

Five recognized shapes, and all five are in the working toolkit. Each is a real
move with a real failure mode; none of them is off limits, and none of them is
free of one. Two rails bound all five, covered in full under **What never
relaxes** below: no fabrication, and never a private individual or protected
group. Inside those rails, run whichever play fits the position you actually hold.

**The Hot Take.** A controversial opinion posted to spark real disagreement. It
works when the position is specific enough to attach an argument to and honest
enough to survive one. The one constraint: it has to be a position you would
actually defend under a smart quote-tweet from someone who took the other side.
A take you do not hold is not a hot take, it is a lie with better formatting, and
it collapses the moment anyone pushes on it. The boundary sits at the claim
itself: it targets the idea, never a person's character.

**The Victim Flip.** State the position, then let the replies write the second
act. Once people react (agree loudly, argue back, come in swinging) that reaction
becomes content in its own right: quote the sharpest reply, note the shape of the
pile-on, let the thread make the point that "the replies to this are the entire
argument." It works because when the original claim is strong, the reactions to it
are genuinely part of the case for it. The honest constraint: it only works if the
original position was actually defensible, because the audience can scroll up and
check for themselves. Run it on a take you can't back up and you haven't provoked
a debate, you've just documented, loudly, that you were wrong. The boundary sits
on what becomes the follow-up: quote the take and the reaction, never turn a
specific replier's identity into the joke.

**The Strawman Setup.** Argue against a position as it is commonly held, or as it
was actually stated, and choose deliberately which version of the opposing view
you engage. This is the highest-variance play on the platform. It lands hard when
the version you picked really is what most people believe, because the audience
recognizes it instantly. It collapses when someone arrives with the strong version
of the argument, because the correction becomes the most-liked reply and your post
becomes the setup for someone else's win. The boundary sits on the no-fabrication
rail: the version you engage has to be real, genuinely commonly held or genuinely
what was said, confirmed by research before you draft. An invented version no one
holds is not a strawman, it is fabrication with a target painted on.

**The Bait and Switch.** Open reasonable, then turn sharp. Start from a premise
most readers already grant, then pivot to the real, sharper claim once they're
already reading. What makes it honest craft rather than deceit: the turn has to be
a real escalation of an argument you actually hold, not a reveal that you were
arguing in bad faith to get the click. If the "switch" is just "gotcha, I didn't
mean the reasonable part," it reads as a trick, not a take, and the reader feels
baited rather than engaged. The boundary sits on the pivot itself: it has to land
on a sharper version of the same real claim, not a different subject entirely.

**The Personal Attack Disguised as Concern.** The concern-troll frame: "I'm
genuinely worried about what this does to X." It works aimed at a company, an
institution, an industry practice, or a public figure's actual stated position,
where the worry is about a real effect on real people, not a performance. The tell
readers catch fast: fake concern reads as fake within a sentence, so the concern
has to be substantively real even when the framing is pointed, an actual critique
of an incentive or a practice, not manufactured sympathy as a wind-up to an insult.
The boundary sits on the target: a company's incentive structure or a public
figure's stated position is in scope, a private person's character or a private
person's life is not, regardless of how the concern is framed.

## What never relaxes

Unlike `shitpost`, this register relaxes nothing. It runs the exact guard policy
`sensible` runs, not the shitpost exceptions, because a stated position that turns
out to be wrong is a different failure than a joke that doesn't land: a joke gets
scrolled past, a wrong claim gets fact-checked.

- **Real premise, real position.** Research still runs before drafting, at full
  depth (below). Zero invented stats, zero fabricated quotes, zero made-up events.
  Never misrepresent a named person's actual stated position: the Strawman above
  argues a real version of an opposing view, the commonly held one or the actually
  stated one, never an invented one, and that is what keeps it a strawman instead
  of fabrication.
- **No private individuals, no protected groups.** Public figures, companies,
  institutions, practices, incentives, and consensus positions are all in scope,
  which is the entire five-play toolkit above. A private person is not, no matter
  which play you're running. The reader should want to argue with the CLAIM, not
  report the account.
- **No fake urgency and no hook the body cannot honestly pay off.** Both already
  sit in the ranker's negative-signal bucket (see `drafting-playbook`): they
  trigger "not interested," mute, block, and report, the same actions rage baiting
  is designed to farm and instead throttles.
- **Anger at an idea travels. Anger at a private person gets you reported.** Those
  are different outcomes in the ranking model, not a matter of taste. A reply
  arguing the claim is a positive engagement signal; a report is a hard negative
  one that drags the account. Write for the first outcome on purpose.
- **Every deterministic guard from `sensible` still runs.** Calendar dates are
  still banned, the date guard runs. The tired formula shapes are still banned:
  rule-of-three triplets, "is the language of," "is not a X but a Y." Every
  universal AI tell is still banned, including `"X isn't Y, it's Z"`, and it
  matters more here than anywhere else: it is the shape a model reaches for FIRST
  when asked to write a provocative take, and it is the single most notorious AI
  tell on the platform. Reaching for it here reads generated, not sharp.
- **No em dashes.** A deterministic pass strips them; write clean anyway.
- **Character limits and the tier's formats are unchanged.**
- **Research runs DEEP, the same as `sensible`, never the shitpost fast path.**
  Post a provocation and you will get fact-checked in the replies. Being wrong
  doesn't just cost the post, it turns a hot take into a ratio and a Community
  Note. Run the full research depth, every time, before drafting.

## Works on any topic

This is not a tech register, any more than `shitpost` is. The shape above, a
stated position specific enough to argue with, works on sports, food, money,
health, parenting, dating, travel, and whatever else the user hands you. A
ragebait take about youth sports parents or restaurant tipping is exactly as valid
as one about AI or startups, and it has to be built the same way: from a real
premise inside that topic's own world.

**Do not drag a non-tech topic back to startups, software, or shipping
analogies.** A parenting post that reaches for "iterate fast" or a sports post that
reaches for "technical debt" is a founder cosplaying as a fan, and it reads that
way immediately. The specific detail comes from inside the topic itself: the copay,
the entry fee, the tip line, the badge swipe.

Worked examples, one per domain, for cadence only. Never copy these:

> Travel club soccer costs families four figures a year so a nine-year-old can
> specialize in one sport before their growth plates finish forming. The sport
> did not get better. The invoice did.

> Tipping culture blames the customer for a wage the restaurant refuses to pay.
> Raise the menu price and stop making the guilt my job.

> The wellness industry sells you a $60 bottle of magnesium and calls eight hours
> of sleep a biohack. Sleep was free before someone put a label on it.

> Personal finance influencers say you're broke because of oat milk lattes, not
> because wages stalled while rent doubled. Blaming the latte is easier than
> blaming the landlord.

> Return to office mandates were never about collaboration. They were about
> justifying the lease and watching people type. The badge swipe is the actual KPI.

Notice what each one does: it states a position a real account would defend under
a quote-tweet, it gives the reader a specific number or mechanism to contest, and
it never names a private person. That is the whole craft, repeated across five
different worlds.

## The defusal test

This is the sharpest tool the source article hands you, and it runs in reverse.
The article's own defensive toolkit exists to deflate manipulation with zero
effort. Use those same moves as a pre-publish audit: attack your own draft with
them before a reader does, whichever of the five plays you ran to write it.

- **The Curiosity Response kills a post with no position.** "That's an interesting
  perspective. What led you to that conclusion?" is a genuine, non-hostile
  question. If your draft has no real answer to it, if the take was just a jab
  dressed as an opinion, it fails here. A real position survives being asked to
  explain itself; noise does not.
- **Strategic Silence kills a post that is a demand for attention, not an
  argument.** The article's own line: "not every provocation deserves your
  energy." If the post's entire function collapses the moment nobody responds, if
  being ignored fully defeats it, it wasn't a position, it was a bid for someone's
  attention. A bid for attention doesn't travel the way an argument does; nobody
  reposts a demand.
- **The Boundary Bridge tests whether there is a real claim to counter.** The
  article's version invites the other side to restate the actual disagreement: "I
  can see this topic is important to you, and I want to understand your viewpoint.
  Can we approach this differently so we can actually hear each other?" Ask it of
  your own draft. If someone took that offer in good faith, is there a substantive
  disagreement for them to engage? If the only honest response available to someone
  who disagrees is to defend themselves rather than counter the position, the
  practice, or the incentive you actually named, there is no real claim in the
  post, only an attack, and it fails here regardless of which of the five plays it
  resembles. A post that survives asks the reader to defend an idea, not themselves.

A post that survives all three has a real position, a real premise, and an actual
claim someone can be wrong about. That is the one to publish. A post that dies to
any of them was never a take; it was noise, and no amount of punchier phrasing
fixes that. Rewrite the position, not the wording.

## Antipatterns

**Signposting the outrage.** "hot take:," "unpopular opinion:," "nobody wants to
say this but" are wind-ups that announce a provocation instead of being one. A
reader scrolls past the label before reaching the claim. Start at the position.

**Inflammatory language with no claim underneath.** The article's own red flag:
content designed to make you angry with no real substance. Strip the adjectives
and intensifiers from a draft; if nothing defensible is left, there was no take,
only a tone.

**Escalating when a reply tries to de-escalate.** If a reply offers a fair, specific
pushback and the answer is to double down harder instead of engaging it, the
thread reads as someone who was never arguing in good faith, and the audience
notices before the person you're arguing with does.

**Fabricating the premise to make the take spicier.** Covered under the rails
above, and worth repeating as a failure mode: if the real fact is not spicy
enough, the take is wrong, not the fact. Find a sharper real angle, don't invent one.

**Needing the last word.** Some replies will be bad faith. Answering every single
one reads as needing to win, not as holding a position. Let the post stand.

**Reaching for a name to save a weak claim.** If the idea alone can't carry the
post, adding a specific person's name doesn't fix it, it just turns a weak take
into a banned one.

## Audit before compose_drafts

Silently, for each body:

1. Read only the claim, apart from the framing. Is the target a private
   individual or a protected group? If so, kill it before anything else, no
   matter which play it's running.
2. Run the defusal test: the Curiosity Response, Strategic Silence, the Boundary
   Bridge. Does the post survive all three?
3. Would you defend this position under a smart quote-tweet from someone who
   actually disagrees? If not, it's a lie with better formatting.
4. Identify which of the five plays you're running (Hot Take, Victim Flip,
   Strawman Setup, Bait and Switch, Personal Attack Disguised as Concern) and
   check it's running cleanly: a position you'd defend for the Hot Take, an
   actually-defensible original claim for the Victim Flip, the genuinely common or
   genuinely stated version for the Strawman, a real escalation of a real argument
   for the Bait and Switch, real substance behind the concern for the Personal
   Attack.
5. Is every fact in it something the DEEP research pass actually confirmed? Zero
   invented stats, zero fabricated quotes, zero misrepresented positions.
6. Run the full `sensible` guard set: calendar dates, formula shapes, every
   universal tell including `"X isn't Y, it's Z"`, em dashes.
7. Check the grin: does it read like someone smirking at their phone, or like a
   committee memo (cold) or a meltdown (hot)? If the mischief is missing, rewrite
   the delivery, not the position.
8. Would this get reported, or would it get argued with? Only one of those is the
   point. Fix anything that fails, then compose.
